# Mythic Generational Character Continuity

## Purpose

Mythic campaigns outlive individual protagonists. A player character may age, retire, disappear, become incapacitated, or die permanently. The authoritative world continues regardless. The player may continue as an heir, descendant, protégé, ally, faction successor, or entirely new character who enters the same persistent world.

## Core invariant

The campaign is the durable continuity boundary; the player character is one actor within it.

Death does not reset the world.

Succession does not rewrite prior history.

A new protagonist inherits only relationships, property, reputation, knowledge, obligations, enemies, titles, artifacts, or privileges that the authoritative world can justify.

## Generational play

Named characters may:

- form relationships;
- marry or establish other recognized family structures supported by the setting;
- have or adopt children;
- raise wards or protégés;
- create heirs through legal, factional, magical, institutional, or cultural systems;
- age according to Ledger Time;
- change occupation, allegiance, beliefs, wealth, health, appearance, equipment, rank, home, and social position;
- die from age, violence, disease, accident, supernatural consequence, execution, sacrifice, or other authoritative causes.

Children and other successors are persistent characters from the moment they enter canonical world state. They are not spawned retroactively when succession becomes useful.

## Player succession choices

When a player character permanently exits play, the campaign may offer valid successors derived from world state, such as:

1. biological or adopted child;
2. other relative;
3. spouse or partner;
4. protégé, apprentice, squire, or ward;
5. companion or party member;
6. faction or organizational successor;
7. unrelated new character generated into the same world.

The player may choose among eligible successors where the world permits. An unrelated new character remains valid so that a campaign never requires dynasty play.

## What persists across protagonists

The following remain canonical unless later changed by world events:

- geography and infrastructure;
- settlements and businesses;
- NPC identities and ages;
- births and deaths;
- households and family trees;
- faction leadership and membership;
- wars and treaties;
- laws and offices;
- ownership and inheritance;
- roads and trade routes;
- inventories and artifacts;
- discovered and hidden locations;
- destroyed and rebuilt structures;
- debts, favors, crimes, reputations, and legends;
- journals and historical records;
- consequences caused by previous protagonists.

## What does not automatically transfer

A successor does not automatically inherit:

- memories they never learned;
- interpersonal trust earned by another person;
- class levels, powers, skills, or injuries;
- private knowledge;
- faction membership;
- legal authority;
- property;
- debts;
- enemies;
- reputation.

Each of these transfers only if supported by authoritative inheritance, teaching, relationship, law, culture, magic, possession, public reputation, or another valid causal mechanism.

## Inheritance model

Succession should support explicit inheritance records for:

- assets and property;
- currency and businesses;
- titles and offices;
- equipment and artifacts;
- debts and obligations;
- faction standing;
- household dependents;
- unresolved quests/contracts;
- legal claims;
- public reputation or family name;
- secrets or knowledge intentionally transmitted.

Inheritance may be contested. Multiple heirs, creditors, governments, factions, enemies, wills, customs, and supernatural claims may compete for the same asset or title.

## Memory and knowledge continuity

Knowledge is actor-specific.

A child may know stories about a parent without possessing the parent's private observations.

A successor may inherit journals, maps, letters, artifacts, witnesses, training, or institutional records that grant legitimate knowledge.

Public historical events can become cultural memory. Private events remain private until transmitted or discovered.

The narrator may use world history for dramatic context but must not grant the active protagonist knowledge that their viewpoint does not possess.

## Reputation across generations

World memory distinguishes actor reputation from lineage or institutional reputation.

Examples:

- A city may revere the deceased hero while distrusting the hero's child.
- An enemy faction may target descendants because of a blood feud.
- A family name may open doors even when the current protagonist has done nothing personally.
- A new unrelated protagonist may encounter monuments or propaganda about a former player character.

## Aging and visual continuity

Ledger Time drives age.

Character visual composition must support reusable age/state variants, including where appropriate:

- child;
- adolescent;
- young adult;
- adult;
- older adult;
- elderly;
- scars/injuries;
- occupation/equipment changes;
- wealth/status changes;
- faction affiliation;
- supernatural transformations.

The visual system should preserve recognizable identity across age transitions when an NPC or player character remains the same person.

## Generational simulation fidelity

Not every child or descendant requires full active-character simulation at all times.

Background family members can remain at lower simulation fidelity while preserving identity, age, household, education/training, health, relationships, location, and major events. They promote to higher fidelity when they become locally relevant or a potential player successor.

## Character creation after death

If no existing successor is chosen, the player may create a new character in the existing campaign.

Character creation must ground that character into the present world:

- birthplace/origin must exist or be validly generated;
- age must align with Ledger Time;
- faction/background relationships must be historically plausible;
- starting equipment and wealth require provenance;
- knowledge must match upbringing and experience;
- the new character enters the current world state, not the original campaign seed.

## Gameplay implications

Generational continuity enables:

- dynastic play without making it mandatory;
- long campaigns spanning decades;
- children growing into rivals, allies, rulers, merchants, criminals, heroes, villains, or player characters;
- inherited artifacts with actual provenance;
- blood feuds and family alliances;
- businesses and estates changing hands;
- political offices passing between generations;
- player-created legacies becoming part of world history;
- campaigns continuing after catastrophic player-character death.

## Acceptance scenarios

1. A player character dies. The world state remains unchanged except for the causal effects of the death.
2. The character's adult child is eligible as a successor and enters play with only justified inheritance and knowledge.
3. The player instead creates an unrelated new character who begins in the same evolved world.
4. A child NPC ages into adulthood over Ledger Time while preserving identity and family history.
5. A former protagonist's business passes through an authoritative inheritance process rather than resetting ownership.
6. An artifact owned by a deceased protagonist can be inherited, stolen, lost, auctioned, seized, or buried based on subsequent world events.
7. A successor encounters NPCs whose attitudes distinguish the successor from the predecessor while accounting for lineage reputation.
8. A new protagonist can discover records, monuments, rumors, or consequences produced by a prior player character without automatically possessing private knowledge.

## Product principle

> The player may change characters. The campaign does not change worlds.

Mythic should make the passage of generations feel like accumulated history rather than a New Game Plus reset.
